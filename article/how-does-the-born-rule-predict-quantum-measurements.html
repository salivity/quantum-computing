How Does the Born Rule Predict Quantum Measurements?

The Born rule is the fundamental postulate of quantum mechanics that links the mathematical description of a quantum state to the concrete probabilities of measurement outcomes. In quantum computing, it explains how the complex probability amplitudes of a qubit or multi-qubit register translate into the classical bits observed when a circuit is measured. By stating that the probability of observing a specific basis state equals the squared magnitude of its corresponding amplitude, the Born rule serves as the essential bridge between quantum superposition and classical readout.

Before measurement, a single qubit exists in a superposition of two computational basis states, typically denoted as |0> and |1>. Mathematically, this state is expressed as a linear combination: |psi> = alpha|0> + beta|1>, where alpha and beta are complex numbers called probability amplitudes. Unlike classical probabilities, which must be real numbers between zero and one, these amplitudes contain both magnitude and phase information, allowing quantum states to interfere constructively and destructively during computation.

When a measurement is performed in the computational basis, the quantum state collapses into either |0> or |1>. According to the Born rule, the probability of measuring the state |0> is given by |alpha|^2, and the probability of measuring the state |1> is given by |beta|^2. Because the total probability of all possible outcomes must equal one, the amplitudes must satisfy the normalization condition |alpha|^2 + |beta|^2 = 1. Computationally, the squared magnitude of a complex amplitude is found by multiplying the complex number by its complex conjugate, which eliminates the phase component and yields a non-negative real number.

This principle scales directly to multi-qubit registers. A system of n qubits has 2^n computational basis states, ranging from |00...0> to |11...1>. Each basis state x has an associated complex amplitude c_x, and the Born rule dictates that the probability of observing the classical bitstring x upon measuring all qubits is |c_x|^2. In a more general mathematical formulation using linear algebra, if a measurement outcome is represented by a projection operator P, the probability of that outcome on a state |psi> is calculated as the inner product <psi|P|psi>.

In practical quantum computing, the Born rule dictates how quantum algorithms are designed and executed. Because a single measurement yields only one probabilistic sample from the underlying distribution, quantum computers must run a circuit many times, known as shots, to estimate the probabilities of different outcomes. Quantum algorithms such as Grover's search and the Quantum Fourier Transform use unitary gates to manipulate phase and amplitude so that through interference, the amplitudes of correct solutions grow large while the amplitudes of incorrect outcomes cancel out. When the final measurement is applied, the Born rule ensures that the desired high-amplitude state is observed with high probability.
